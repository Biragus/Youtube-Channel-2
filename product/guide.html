CHAPTER
01
What a digital product really is
You make it once. It costs nothing to copy. And it can sell while you sleep.

Make once, sell forever

A digital product is anything a person can buy and download or open on a screen. No stock, no shipping, no warehouse. The work happens once, when you make it. After that, selling copy number one hundred costs you exactly what copy number one cost: nothing.

That changes the maths of earning money. At a job, you are paid for hours, and you only have so many. A product is paid for results, and it does not get tired.

TypeWhat it isExample
TemplateA ready-made file people fill inA résumé layout, a wedding budget, a menu design
TrackerA spreadsheet that does the maths for youMileage and tax tracker for delivery drivers
GuideStep-by-step instructions to get one result"Pass your driving test first time"
ChecklistA short list that stops people forgetting thingsMoving-house checklist, launch checklist
PlannerA structure for time or goalsNotion study planner for students
Kit / bundleSeveral small pieces that solve one problem togetherThe Callback Kit: word lists + layout + tracker
Mini-courseA few short videos with a handout"Fix your posture in 10 days"

Three myths that keep people at zero

"I'm not an expert." You only need to be two steps ahead of the person you are helping, and to remember what it felt like to be where they are.

"I need an audience first." You need ten buyers. Ten. You can find ten people with a problem by talking to them directly, before you have a single follower.

"Everything has already been made." General things have. "A budget spreadsheet" is everywhere; "a budget spreadsheet for night-shift nurses paid every two weeks" is not. Specific beats general.

CHAPTER
02
Find what hurts
Do not sell what you like. Sell what hurts. People pay to make pain stop, not to collect nice things.

Rule one: sell what hurts

Most first products fail for one reason: they are something the maker enjoys, not something a buyer needs. A beautiful poetry ebook is lovely. A one-page guide that gets a nervous person through their first job interview gets bought.

Pain is easy to spot once you look for it. It sounds like: "I hate doing this." "I never know how to start." "I keep losing track of." "This takes me forever." "I'm scared of getting it wrong."

The pain inventory

Look at your life through four lenses. Write at least five lines under each, or let Claude interview you (chapter 11, step 1).

  1. Your work. What is annoying, slow or confusing in your job, past or present? What did you have to figure out on your own?
  2. Problems you have solved. Debt you paid off, weight you lost, a visa you got, a landlord you dealt with, an exam you passed. Every solved problem is a map for someone still lost.
  3. What people ask you. If friends keep asking you the same question, that question is a product.
  4. Groups you belong to. Drivers, nurses, students, new parents, gamers, gym beginners, people who rent. Groups share pains, and they talk to each other.

Score your ideas

Give each idea a score from 1 to 5 on four questions. Add them up. The highest total wins, not the one you like most.

QuestionWhat a 5 looks like
How much does it hurt?It costs them money, time or sleep, regularly
Can you reach them?You know where these people gather, online or in person
Will they pay?They already spend money around this problem
Do you understand it?You lived it, or you fixed it for yourself

03
Choose the format
Small, specific and quick to use. Your first product should solve one problem in under an hour.

Match the pain to the format

Listen to how people describe the problem. The words tell you what to make.

They say…Make…
"I don't know how to do it."A step-by-step guide or checklist
"I keep losing track."A tracker or spreadsheet
"It takes me forever."A template or a kit
"I never know what to say."Scripts or message templates
"I start and then give up."A planner, a challenge, or a short course

Keep it small

Your first product is not your life's work. It is a test. Small products are faster to make, easier to explain in one sentence, cheaper to buy, and easier to finish. A buyer should be able to open it and get a result the same day.

  • One type of person
  • One problem
  • One clear result

The one-sentence promise

Before you make anything, write this sentence. If you cannot fill it in, the idea is not clear yet.

[Product] helps [who] [get what result] without [the pain].

Examples:

  • The Driver's Ledger helps self-employed delivery drivers track miles, fuel and tax in two taps, without a shoebox of receipts.
  • The Callback Kit helps job seekers get interviews by writing résumés the hiring software can read, without rewriting everything from scratch.
Today's action: write your one-sentence promise (chapter 11, step 2). Read it out loud. If it sounds vague, make it more specific.
CHAPTER
04
Check it before you build it
Ten conversations will save you ten weekends. Ask about their problem, not your idea.

The ten-conversation test

The biggest waste in this whole process is building something for a month and then finding out nobody wanted it in that form. You fix that by talking to ten real people from your group before you build.

Where to find them

  • People you already know, online groups (Facebook, Reddit, Discord, forums: read the rules, never spam), and places your group already gathers.

What to say

Do not pitch. Nobody tells the truth about an idea when you are asking them to like it. Ask about their life instead.

Hey! Quick question, I'm working on something for [group]. How do you currently handle [the problem]? What's the most annoying part of it? Have you ever paid for anything to make it easier? What was it? If something fixed [the most annoying part], what would it look like?

Green flags and red flags

Green: keep going

  • They talk for a long time without being pushed
  • They describe a workaround they hate
  • They have already paid for something similar
  • They ask "when can I get it?"

Red: change something

  • "Yeah, that's a cool idea" and nothing else
  • They have never tried to solve it
  • They cannot remember the last time it happened
  • Everyone wants something different

Write down their exact words (chapter 11, step 3). "I just want it on my phone, two taps, max" becomes your product, your sales page and your videos.

CHAPTER
05
Build it in a weekend
Version one should be useful, not perfect. Ugly and finished beats beautiful and in your head.

The weekend build

Give yourself one weekend. A deadline stops you polishing forever. If it takes longer, the product is too big: cut it in half.

WhenDo this
Saturday morningWrite the outline: every step, section or tab, in order. Use the words from your ten conversations.
Saturday afternoonBuild the core. Fill in every section roughly. Do not design anything yet.
Sunday morningAdd one fully worked example. People learn from examples more than instructions.
Sunday afternoonMake it clean and readable. Then test it with two real people and watch them use it.

Free tools that are enough

  • Google Sheets for trackers, calculators and budgets.
  • Google Docs for guides, scripts and checklists. Export as PDF.
  • Canva (free plan) for planners, printables, covers and product images.
  • Notion for planners and templates people duplicate into their own account.
  • Claude to write, design and check all of it with you (chapter 11).

A shape that works for guides

  1. The problem, in their words, so they feel understood.
  2. The result they will have at the end.
  3. The steps, numbered, one action each.
  4. An example of someone doing it.
  5. A checklist they can tick off.

For templates and trackers: an instructions tab in plain language, a filled-in example so they can see what "done" looks like, and a blank version ready to use.

Before you call it finished

  • Two people from your group used it without your help
  • It opens properly on a phone
  • No spelling mistakes in the title or first page
  • It delivers the promise from your one-sentence promise
  • Your name or brand is on it
Today's action: book your build weekend in your calendar. Tell one person, so you have to do it.
CHAPTER
06
Price it
Price the result, not the number of pages. A one-page script that wins a pay rise is worth more than a 200-page ebook.

How much to charge

For a first, small product, most prices land between $7 and $29. Low enough to be an easy yes, high enough to be taken seriously. Free is not a price: people value what they pay for, and free downloads mostly sit unopened.

Three questions

  1. What does the problem cost them? If it costs a driver $300 a year in missed tax deductions, $9 is nothing.
  2. What do they already pay for nearby? If they spend $15 on a book about it, you can sit near $15.
  3. How fast is the result? Faster results justify higher prices.
ProductTypical starting range
Checklist, single template, scripts$5 – $12
Tracker or spreadsheet with maths built in$9 – $19
Kit or bundle of several pieces$12 – $29
Detailed guide with worksheets$15 – $35
Short video course$29 – $99

These ranges are common starting points, not rules. Your group and your result matter more.

Launch price

Start a little lower for your first twenty buyers and say so honestly: "Launch price for the first 20 people." It gives early buyers a reason to act now, and you can raise the price once you have reviews.

Today's action: pick a price. Write it next to your one-sentence promise. Do not change it for two weeks.
CHAPTER
07
Open your shop
You do not need a website. A product page on a selling platform is enough to take your first payment today.

Setting up on a selling platform

Platforms like Payhip, Gumroad and Stan handle the payment, deliver the file automatically and send the receipt. Most charge a small percentage per sale and nothing upfront. The steps below use Payhip as the example; the others work in a very similar way. Menus change over time, so if a button has moved, check the platform's help pages.

  1. Create your account and connect a way to get paid (PayPal and/or Stripe).
  2. Add a product and choose a digital download.
  3. Upload your file. For PDFs, turn on PDF stamping if available, so each copy carries the buyer's email.
  4. Write the title: the result, not the format. "Get Interviews: The Callback Kit" beats "Résumé Template Pack".
  5. Write the description using the formula below.
  6. Add images: a cover, plus two or three images showing the inside.
  7. Set the price and, if you want, a launch coupon.
  8. Buy it yourself with a 100% coupon to test that the download arrives.

The product page formula

  1. Headline: your one-sentence promise.
  2. The problem: two or three lines in their words.
  3. What's inside: a short list. Say what each part does, not just what it is.
  4. Who it's for (and who it's not for). Honesty builds trust.
  5. Pictures of the inside, so they know exactly what they get.
  6. Questions people ask, answered in one line each.
  7. Your refund policy, stated plainly.

CHAPTER
08
Your first ten sales
Forget going viral. You need ten people. Show the problem every day, and point to the fix.

Show the problem, every day

People do not search for "templates". They search for answers to the thing that is hurting them. So do not make videos about your product. Make videos about their problem, and let the product be the obvious next step.

The 30-second video formula

SecondsWhat happensExample
0–3Name the pain"Why your résumé never gets a reply."
3–20Show one useful thing, for freeShow the before and after of one line
20–30One line pointing to the full fix"The full kit is in my bio."

Post one every day on TikTok, Reels and Shorts; the same video works on all three. Most early videos get few views. That is normal.

Twelve video ideas for any product

  • The mistake everyone makes with…
  • Before and after
  • "I did this for five years so you don't have to"
  • Reacting to a real question you were asked
  • Fixing one thing live
  • The myth about…
  • What I wish I knew when I started
  • A day in the life of [your group]
  • Three signs you need…
  • A customer's result (with permission)
  • Your own failure story
  • "Steal my exact…"

Do not forget the direct route

Go back to the ten people from chapter 4. They told you about their problem. Tell them you made something for it, and offer them the launch price. Some of your first sales will come from these messages, not from videos.

Track four numbers

Once a week, write down: views, clicks to your page, sales, and what people said. It tells you exactly what to fix (chapter 9).

CHAPTER
09
When it goes wrong
It will. Zero sales, angry customers, a broken file. How you respond is what people remember.

No sales after two weeks

Do not delete it. Find the leak. Look at your four numbers from chapter 8.

What you seeWhat it meansWhat to do
Hardly any viewsNobody is seeing itPost more, in more places. Talk to people directly.
Views, but no clicksThe message is not landingName the pain more clearly in the first three seconds.
Clicks, but no salesThe page or offer is not convincingBetter inside images, clearer promise, check the price.
Sales, but refundsThe product does not deliver the promiseAsk why, fix it, and email past buyers the update.

Refunds

Give them, quickly and politely. Fighting over a few dollars costs you far more in reputation. Ask one question: "What did you hope it would do?" The answer is free research.

An angry public comment

Do not delete it, and do not argue. Reply in public, calmly, and offer to fix their problem personally. Other people are watching how you handle it. A complaint handled well can sell more than any advert.

A mistake in your product

Tell every buyer, fix it fast, send the fixed version, and say sorry once, clearly. Most people forgive mistakes. Almost nobody forgives being ignored.

THE STORY

"I bought your kit. I still got rejected." Kai could have deleted it. He replied in public, rewrote her résumé himself for free, and nine days later she posted a photo of her new office badge. That post sold more than anything he ever wrote about himself.

Today's action: decide your refund rule now, before anyone asks, and write it on your product page.
CHAPTER

Product two and three
Your customers will tell you what to make next. Every question they ask is your next product.

From one product to a small shop

The hardest product is the first. After that, you have three things you did not have before: buyers, their questions, and proof. Use all three.

Listen for the next question

Keep a note of every question a buyer asks. When the same one appears three times, that is product two. Kai's résumé kit buyers asked "what do I say in the interview?" and "how do I ask for more money?" That became an interview guide and a salary script.

Build a ladder

StepExamplePrice
FreeA one-page checklist, in return for an email address$0
StarterYour first product$7 – $19
BundleProducts one, two and three together, at a discount$29 – $49
BiggerA course, or personal help$99+

Collect emails

Followers belong to the app. Email addresses belong to you. Most platforms collect the buyer's email automatically; write to your list when you have something genuinely useful or new.

Raise your price

Once you have around twenty sales and a few good reviews, raise the price a little. If sales hold, raise it again later.

Today's action: start a "questions" note. Every question a buyer or viewer asks goes in it.

The Claude checklist

It has never been this easy

A few years ago, making a digital product meant learning design software, writing every word yourself, and paying an editor to make your videos. Today you can do every part of it by talking to an AI assistant. This chapter shows you exactly how, using Claude.

You do not need to know how to code. You do not need design skills. You copy a prompt from this chapter, paste it into Claude, fill in the parts in [brackets], and answer Claude's questions. That is it.

How it works: copy the prompt, paste it into Claude, answer its questions, and tell it what to change. Plain words, like texting a friend.

Before you start

  • A Claude account. Use Claude in your browser or the Claude app. A paid plan gives you more room for long projects like a video.
  • Claude Code for the videos. It is the part of Claude that can write and run code for you, which is how the animation gets made. You never have to read the code.
  • An ElevenLabs account for the narration voice, music and sound effects. Connect it to Claude in Claude's connectors settings so Claude can use it for you. ElevenLabs charges for credits; a full video's voice usually costs a few dollars.
  • A shop account on a selling platform such as Payhip, so you can take payments.

Apps and menus change over time. If something has moved, ask Claude: "Where do I find [the setting] now?"

Three rules that make it work

  1. Tell it about you. Claude is only as specific as you are. Your job, your problems, your people.
  2. Ask to see it first. Say "show me a sample before you do the whole thing". It saves time and money.
  3. Give notes like a director. "The music is too loud at 1:35, make it a third." Specific notes get exact fixes.
Part A · Find your product

Steps 1 to 4: the idea

☐ Step 1 · Find what hurts

Interview me to find a digital product idea I could sell. Ask me one question at a time about my job, problems I have solved in my life, what people ask me for help with, and groups I belong to. When you have enough, give me 5 product ideas and score each from 1 to 5 on: how much the problem hurts, how easy the buyers are to reach, whether they already pay for solutions, and how well I know the problem. Recommend the best one and tell me why.

☐ Step 2 · Make it specific

For the idea "[your idea]", write my one-sentence promise in this form: [Product] helps [who] [get what result] without [the pain]. Then give me 3 versions, each more specific than the last, and suggest the best format (guide, template, tracker, checklist, kit or scripts) and a price.

☐ Step 3 · Check it with real people

Write a short, friendly message I can send to people in [your group] to ask about [the problem] without selling anything. Then list 10 places online where these people talk to each other, and the rules I should follow so I do not come across as spam.

Send it to ten people (chapter 4). Then paste their answers back in:

☐ Step 4 · Pick the price

Part B · Make the product

Steps 5 to 9: the product and the shop

☐ Step 5 · Write it

Write the full [format] for "[your promise]". Use these exact phrases from my buyers: [paste]. Plain language, short sentences, numbered steps, one real example per section, and a checklist at the end. Start with a detailed outline and wait for my OK before writing the rest.

☐ Step 6 · Design it

Turn this into a beautifully designed, branded PDF. Style: [your colours and mood, e.g. dark green and black, bold and modern]. Add a cover, a contents page and a designed opener for each chapter. Make sure no page is left half empty. Show me previews of every page before you finish.

For a spreadsheet or tracker, ask instead: "Build this as a Google Sheets template with an instructions tab, a filled-in example and a blank copy."

☐ Step 7 · Make the shop images

Make 4 square images for my shop page, in the same style as the PDF: 1) the cover as a 3D book mockup, 2) a look inside with three pages fanned out, 3) a "what you'll learn" list, 4) the most useful page up close.

☐ Step 8 · Write the shop page

Write my product page for [Payhip / your platform]: a title that names the result, the price, a description that starts with the buyer's problem, a list of what is inside and what each part does, who it is for and not for, five FAQs with one-line answers, and a plain refund policy. Do not promise any income.

☐ Step 9 · Publish

  • Paste the page into your shop, upload the PDF and the 4 images
  • Turn on PDF stamping and email collection if your platform has them
  • Do a test purchase with a 100% coupon, then delete the coupon

Part C · Make the videos

Steps 10 to 16: animated story videos

Use Claude Code for this part. Short story videos are what bring buyers to your page (chapter 8). Claude can write them, voice them, animate them and edit them.

☐ Step 10 · Create your character

Design an original cartoon stickman character drawn entirely in code, with a "before" outfit (struggling) and an "after" outfit (successful), a set of facial expressions, and 5 supporting characters (a doubter, a family member, a boss, a friend who believes in him, a mentor). Render one character sheet image so I can approve the look.

☐ Step 11 · Write the script

Write an animated story script, about 1,550 words (8 to 9 minutes), spoken to "you". Start straight in the story, no intro. Story: someone broke and stuck in [their situation] turns [their own experience] into a digital product: [your product]. Structure: situation, the problem, a first try that fails, a small start that works, proof, making the product, slow first sales, a hard moment handled well, the new life, the people who doubted come back, a short close. One shot per line, with what is on screen and the sound. Last line: "If you want to learn how to do it, the step-by-step guide is linked in my bio. And subscribe."

☐ Step 12 · Give it a voice

Using ElevenLabs, find 3 deep, calm narrator voices that suit this story and generate the first 20 seconds with each, so I can pick one. Do not generate the full narration until I approve.

☐ Step 13 · Music and sound effects

Generate background music in four moods (sad, curious, driving, warm) and the sound effects this script needs. Keep effects quiet and the music well under the voice.

☐ Step 14 · Animate and render

Animate every shot in code using my character, with a new shot about every 5 seconds, timed to the narration. Mix the voice, music and sound effects and render a 1080p MP4. Before the full render, show me one still of every shot so I can check them.

☐ Step 15 · Watch it and give notes

At 0:35 the clock sound is too loud, make it a fifth. At 1:32 the caption gets cut off, move it up. At 6:10 the pause is too long, shorten it. Fix all of these, then render again.

☐ Step 16 · Make it remember

Save everything we agreed (the voice, the sound levels, the style and my notes) in a project notes file, so every future video starts from these settings.

From now on, your next video starts with one message: "Write the next video about [new story], same style and settings as last time."

Then post it, put your product link in the description and your bio, and do it again next week. Your first video may take a few rounds of notes; every one after that gets faster.
